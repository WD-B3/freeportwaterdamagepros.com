How to Read a Water Damage Scope of Work in Freeport, IL

24/7 Emergency Line(815) 314-9281

By the Freeport Water Damage Pros team · Updated November 9, 2026

A scope of work is the plain-language list of what a crew will do, where and with what equipment. It is not the price, though it drives the price. Reading it carefully before work begins is the cheapest protection a Freeport homeowner has, because most disputes later come from something the scope left unclear.

Think of it as a map of the job. If a room, a wall or a step is missing from the map, nobody has agreed to do it. If a line says only "dry structure," you cannot tell how long, with what, or how anyone will know it worked.

The sections a good scope has

Parts of a water damage scope
SectionWhat it should sayWhat to look for
Affected areasEach room, floor and surface by nameSquare footage or dimensions
Water categoryCategory 1, 2 or 3 and how it was decidedSource of the water stated
ExtractionHow standing water is removedMethod and areas covered
DemolitionMaterials removed and their quantityBaseboard, drywall height, carpet pad
Drying planEquipment type, count and placementDaily readings and a drying goal
CleaningSurfaces and contents treatedAntimicrobial use described
ExclusionsWhat is not includedRebuild, paint, mold work, electrical
ChangesHow added work is approvedWritten change orders

Water category and why it matters

The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard groups water into three categories. Category 1 comes from a sanitary source. Category 2 has significant contamination. Category 3 is grossly contaminated, such as sewage or floodwater. A scope that never names the category is hiding a decision that changes the whole job.

The category decides which materials come out. A clean supply line leak may let you keep carpet and dry the pad in place. Sewage means porous materials come out, as described on our sewage backup cleanup page.

Drying plan details

The drying section should name the kinds of equipment, such as air movers and dehumidifiers, and give counts per room. It should say that technicians will take moisture readings at set times and keep a log. It should also state a drying goal, usually the moisture level of a similar dry material in your own home.

Without a goal, "drying" can end when the crew gets tired or when the equipment is needed elsewhere. With a goal, you and the adjuster can see when it is done. Our structural drying page explains what the readings mean.

Questions to bring to the walk-through

  • Which rooms are included and which are not?
  • What is the water category and why?
  • How many air movers and dehumidifiers will run, and for how many days?
  • What readings mean the structure is dry?
  • What happens if the goal is not met on the planned date?
  • Who handles the rebuild?

A company that welcomes these questions is showing you how it works. A company that avoids them is showing you something too.

Photos belong with the scope

A scope is stronger when it is paired with dated photos of each affected area, the moisture readings and a sketch of the rooms. If you ever need to explain the work to an adjuster, a buyer or another contractor, that package answers the question without a long phone call.

What to do if a scope skips safety

A good scope names the water category and says how electrical hazards, contaminated materials and structural concerns will be handled. If it never mentions safety, ask. Illinois consumer guidance also advises getting the contract in writing before work begins.

Line items that signal vague work

  • "Misc. demo" with no quantity
  • "Dry as needed" with no goal
  • A scope that mentions only one room when the water clearly spread
  • No mention of contents, furniture moves or pack-out
  • No exclusions list at all
  • Equipment listed with no days of use

A vague line is not proof of bad faith. It is a question to ask before signing. If the answer comes back in writing, the scope improves and so does your position if the job changes.

Mitigation scope versus repair scope

Many companies write two scopes. The first covers mitigation, which is stopping the damage by extracting water, removing wet materials and drying. The second covers repair, which is the rebuild. They may come from the same company or from different ones, and they may be billed separately.

Ask who is responsible for which, and where the handoff happens. Gaps between the two are where leftover moisture, missing baseboards and unfinished walls tend to end up. A scope for a ceiling loss, for example, should say who patches and paints once the ceiling is dry, as covered under ceiling water damage repair.

Why local drainage shows up in scopes

An EPA case study on Freeport describes separate sanitary and storm sewers and a history of wet-weather sewer problems tied to the city's varied terrain. It also records a hundred-year rain in 2001 that flooded the Homer Street area.

If your loss involves a floor drain or basement backup, the scope should say which line the water came from and which materials were treated as contaminated. That detail matters to your insurer, and it is easy to leave out when a crew is rushing.

Signing, changes and records

The Illinois Attorney General says a written contract is required for home repair work over $1,000 and that oral promises should be put in writing. Treat the scope as part of that contract, and ask that change orders be written and signed before extra work starts.

Keep a copy of the scope with your photos and your insurance claim number. If you live in Lena or Orangeville, the same habits apply. A scope on paper beats a verbal promise every time.

Common questions

Who writes the scope of work?

The restoration company usually writes it after inspecting the loss. Your insurer may write its own version, and the two can differ.

Can the scope change after work starts?

Yes, when walls open or readings show more water. The contract should explain how changes are approved in writing.

What if the adjuster scope is smaller than the company scope?

Ask both to explain the difference line by line. Moisture readings and photos are the best evidence.

Does a scope include rebuilding?

Not always. Many scopes cover only mitigation. Ask whether rebuild is included or handled separately.
